Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-1.7B-Base

Idea: turn speech into a stream of integer codes (like words), then train a Qwen3-style language model to continue that stream given text. A neural audio codec (the speech tokenizer) converts between waveforms and codes, so generation becomes pure next-token prediction.

2. What happens in time: one 80 ms frame

sequenceDiagram
  autonumber
  participant P as Prompt builder
  participant T as Talker 28 layers
  participant C as Code predictor 5 layers
  participant D as Tokenizer decoder
  P->>T: speaker vector, reference text and codes, target text (once)
  loop every 80 ms frame
    T->>T: attend over everything so far, produce a hidden state
    T->>T: codec_head, sample codebook 0 (semantic token)
    T->>C: hidden state plus codebook 0 embedding
    loop codebooks 1 to 15
      C->>C: predict next codebook with its own head, embed it, continue
    end
    C->>T: 16 embeddings summed, next input (with next text token)
    C->>D: 16 codes for this frame
    D-->>D: 1920 samples at 24 kHz = 80 ms of audio
  end
  T->>T: stops when it emits the end-of-speech codec token

This is why Qwen is slow on our GPU: the Talker runs once per frame, and every frame also runs 15 sequential code-predictor steps. 12.5 frames per second of audio means over 200 small sequential model calls per second of speech.

  • Semantic vs acoustic codebooks: codebook 0 is trained (with a WavLM teacher) to carry what is said and how; the 15 residual codebooks each add the detail the previous ones missed (timbre, breath, fine texture). That is why the Talker only has to "decide" codebook 0 with its big model, and a small network fills the rest.
  • Fully causal: the encoder and decoder only look at the past, so audio can stream with about 100 ms first-packet latency (paper). Trained as a GAN codec with a multi-scale mel loss.
  • The ceiling: 16 codebooks x 2048 entries at 12.5 Hz is a lossy bottleneck. Whatever the codec can't represent (notably above 12 kHz) can never appear in the output.

Each SE-Res2Net block = a Res2Net (7 small 64-channel convs on 8 channel groups, multi-scale) followed by squeeze-and-excitation (512 to 128 to 512 channel reweighting). The structure (TDNN, SE-Res2Net, MFA, attentive pooling) is the ECAPA-TDNN recipe; I identified it from the tensor names and shapes, the paper only calls it a "learnable speaker encoder, trained jointly". 12M parameters. It sees the whole reference clip and squeezes it into one vector, so it captures timbre but not moment-to-moment delivery. The in-context path (reference codes plus transcript) is what carries prosody.

VoxCPM2

Idea: don't use discrete tokens at all. Compress speech to a short sequence of continuous latent patches, let a language model decide what each patch should contain at a coarse level, and let a small diffusion model paint the exact patch. Then decode the latents at a higher sample rate than the audio was encoded at.

sequenceDiagram
  autonumber
  participant L as LocEnc
  participant T as TSLM

  participant D as LocDiT
  participant V as AudioVAE decoder

    L->>T: embedding of the previous patch (acoustic history)
    T->>F: hidden state from reading text plus history
    F->>R: quantised skeleton, fused with history
    R->>D: residual acoustic state (mu_res)
    F->>D: skeleton (mu_sem)

    D->>L: the new patch becomes history

Half as many LM steps per second as Qwen (6.25 vs 12.5), but each one also runs the 12-layer diffusion network 10 times. That is why speeds end up similar on our GPU.

3. AudioVAE V2: why 16 kHz in can mean 48 kHz out

  classDef io fill:#173d2d,stroke:#3ecf8e,color:#e6e8ee

  • The arithmetic: 16,000 / 640 = 25 latent frames per second; 25 x 1920 = 48,000 samples per second. The decoder simply upsamples more than the encoder downsampled.
  • Implicit super-resolution: training pairs 16 kHz inputs with 48 kHz targets, so the decoder learns to generate plausible 8-24 kHz content from the 0-8 kHz information in the latent. The 64-number latent per 40 ms can't hold exact high-frequency detail; the decoder fills in typical high-frequency detail for the voice and phonemes. It is not recovering your real 8-24 kHz, because it never saw it.
  • Why 16 kHz in: keeps the LM sequence short (6.25 steps/s) and lets them reuse their huge 16 kHz speech corpus.
  • The sample-rate FiLM: the decoder is told which output rate to produce (sr_bin_boundaries = 20,000 / 30,000 / 40,000 Hz). We pass 48 kHz, the top bin.

  • Flow matching trains the network to predict the velocity that carries noise to the true latent along a straight path; at inference we integrate that velocity with 10 Euler steps (our inference_timesteps=10; more steps = slower, possibly cleaner).
  • Classifier-free guidance (cfg 2.0): run the network with and without the language-model conditioning, then extrapolate: v = v_uncond + 2.0 x (v_cond - v_uncond). Stronger guidance follows text and style harder but can sound over-driven. The report says 1.5 to 3.0 works. It also uses sway sampling (more steps at high noise) and CFG-Zero* by default. This doubles the compute per step.
  • Why a diffusion head at all: averaging over possible continuations (what a plain regression head does) makes muffled speech. Drawing a sample from a learned distribution keeps sharp, natural detail, at the cost of speed and some run-to-run variability, which is why the model card says to generate 1 to 3 takes.

What this means for our pipeline

  • Bandwidth: Qwen's ceiling is the codec (24 kHz, 12 kHz of content). VoxCPM2's top end is generated by the decoder from 0-8 kHz information. That is why it has energy to 14-16 kHz in our measurement, and also why it may not match your real 8-16 kHz detail.
  • Fidelity to your voice: Qwen gets a dedicated speaker vector and an in-context reference. VoxCPM2 gets only the reference audio as context. My expectation (untested here) is that Qwen's identity is the more stable of the two, and that VoxCPM2 depends more on the quality and delivery of the reference clip.
  • Controllability: only VoxCPM2 accepts a plain-language style description alongside a cloned voice. Qwen would need its CustomVoice or VoiceDesign variants, which don't clone your voice directly.
  • Speed: both are autoregressive and sequential. Qwen: 12.5 steps/s x (28-layer Talker + 15 code-predictor steps). VoxCPM2: 6.25 steps/s x (28-layer TSLM + 8-layer RALM + 10 x 12-layer diffusion, doubled for CFG). Neither is fast without optimised kernels (flash attention, CUDA graphs), which this ROCm setup lacks.
  • Run-to-run variation: both sample randomly (top-k sampling vs diffusion noise). Generating several takes and picking the best is a legitimate quality lever for either.
  • Ideas this suggests: (1) VoxCPM2 only hears the reference up to 8 kHz, so what to optimise in the reference is its delivery (pace, energy, tone), not its bandwidth; (2) try voice-design style strings and a library of short reference clips per emotion; (3) try more diffusion steps or lower CFG to trade speed and over-driven sound; (4) fine-tune the small parts (LoRA on LocDiT/RALM) on your own voice.
